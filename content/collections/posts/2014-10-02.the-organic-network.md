The Internet we've built today is very static. Every day we add more to it by publishing new content or sending around some emails. Some data changes fairly frequently, such as you mail spool or IM status. However, for the most part, the underlying platform of interconnected nodes is rigid. By rigid, I mean that your "nodes" or computers you use to access the network, don't change much. Your home computer might always be on and connected through the same collection of static subnets. You might even have your own subnet of machines around your house. This is so common that toys such as the Wii, XBox 360, and PS3 will likely be connected to your home's subnet. These are the devices you regularly use to interface the existing network we call Internet.

Those devices, although interconnected, are rather rigid themselves. Your smartphone, laptop, pc, Wii, and mp3 player might all have different collections of data. We're getting better at syncing between devices. You'll likely check the same email account with your laptop as you would with your smartphone. This is useful, but we can do better. We can do much better.

Consider for a moment the data cloud created by a bit torrent network. Certain bits of data are spread over many hosts in such a way that you can retrieve a large and popular file for many different nodes of the network simultaneously. This brilliant technology served to turn our understanding of media rights upside down. It was good stuff. Nodes would come and go yet the integrity of a larger computing process is never hindered.

Also consider distributed compiling systems such as Incredibuild. Compiling your C++ project might take 30 minutes on your out of date computer. However, with Incredibuild, you could spread your compiling tasks out to several machines on the network. The other machines will only help if they have nothing better to do, which usually is the case. Your compile times can easily drop by a fraction relative to the number of machines on the network. It really is incredible.

The new network would not have lots of static nodes like Internet. Instead of having dedicated Web servers, an active user would run their own server from their current location. You would carry with you some kind of mass storage device not entirely unlike a USB key or SD card. This device might be a computer and communications device itself, not entirely unlike a smart phone.

To "connect" to this network, you always require you device. It's like a key... It's also like your wallet... It's hard to say exactly how we'll use it and what it will do for us. However, you won't worry about owning a terminal to the network anymore. You can own one if you wish, but why bother? Every coffee shop and government building would have various types of terminals. Some terminals are engineered for certain types of activities. A gaming terminal would be very different from an accounting terminal.

This portable device you carry around is so darn useful, you'd consider having it permanently attached to you body. While you'll probably have a special pocket on your clothes just for your device, we're still a long way off from cyborgs. Lets get back to the network itself.

When you walk out in the woods with your personal device and can no longer access the network, you are your own single node network. When you walk past someone on the forest trail, you might for a moment be a two node network. Of course, at any time you wish, you could turn your device off. In fact, you might be the kind of person who just carries out a storage device with not communication or computational ability at all. It still interfaces with all terminal devices from telephones to videogames.

We're already building this type of technology. If you want, you can carry your Wiimote around with you and use the same Mii characters on every Wii you play on. However, even on a machines as well designed as the Wii, managing your data this way was designed in as an afterthought, not a primary user function.

Because of this design, the new network will have many dead nodes where no one is plugged in. These nodes might be specialized machines of many different kinds but they all communicate through a common set of protocols. For any node to become "active", someone has to be logged into it. Unlike Internet, this new network will not be a "fire and forget" network like the Web. To simulate a real connection, Web app developers use Javascript to poll back to a server on a regular cycle. This new network is much more like Bulletin Board Systems where you connected directly to the BBS through a telephone line. If someone picked up the phone in the house, snap - you're offline.

If you disconnect from a terminal, it automatically becomes a dormant node on the network. Dormant nodes can be made available to donate processing power to the network. The network itself would be evolutionarily engineered to optimize and take advantage of the collective data manipulation potential of the network.

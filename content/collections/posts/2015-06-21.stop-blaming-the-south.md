Many in the United States of America <a href="http://www.cnn.com/2015/06/21/politics/south-carolina-confederate-flag-debate/">are calling for the removal of the Confederate flag from all government sites</a>. I entirely agree with this. The flag hurts people, we should put it in a museum. However, in the wake of this appropriate outcry I find myself saddened by the inappropriate generalization about the South. Mostly, it seems to stem from debate around that flag.

I recently polled my social network asking them how they were introduced to the Confederate flag. The responses were polarized in the extreme. For those who saw it as a symbol of racism, stories included seeing the flag on the clothing of inner-city skinheads. For those who saw it as a cultural symbol, stories included a high school where the sports teams were the "Rebels" and they few the flag as their symbol. Messed up, right? And these kids are supposed to grow up and relate to each other in the real world?

Personally, I have a whole bunch of mixed connections. I was born in a small town in Texas. I moved to various states growing up, but I also graduated from high school and undergraduate college in Texas. I never considered myself a southerner but I did think of myself and as native Texan. My introduction to the Confederate flag? I watched the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dukes_of_Hazzard">Dukes of Hazzard</a> when I was a toddler!

The political issue attached to the Confederate Flag is racism and it's association with our history of slavery. Ironically, most of whites in the South, even the most racist among them, are not kin to slave owners. Their privilege was a construct fabricated by the wealthy a few centuries ago. You can see when this happened, that time in history when suddenly slavery in the United States went from multi-racial to being defined very specifically as a single race. These were ugly times, but understanding them is important. One should also understand from where, how, and why the wealthy suddenly had access to cheaper and plentiful forced labor, but that's for another narrative. This narrative is more about me.

I am the only member of my immediately family born in Texas. My father comes from Virginia and my mother from Mississippi. My sister was born in Oregon, which as a side note has a very racist history and has nothing to do with the South. My mom's mom settled in Texas and much of my mother's extended family has roots there. Most of them were immigrants from around the time of World War 2. I am very much German with some Czech. My father, on the other hand, doesn't know as much about his heritage. We know his father had some connection to England. The only thing he knew about his mother was the Native American half or quarter, we're not sure. I've called many extended relatives and have only been able to pick up bits and pieces. However, nowhere in there do I see reason to believe any of my ancestors owned slaves until, maybe, back in England at Dudley's Castle - and this would be a remarkable connection.

I was not mindful of slavery or racism being associating the Confederate Flag. Instead, I truly did associate it with many things I do love about Southern culture. Seriously, my favorite food is fried chicken.  When I think of that flag, I think of sun-tea, birds, and a porch swing.

I understand why it needs to go away, but I also understand that there IS a deep cultural positive attachment that will die with it. I know that flag should go, but what you may not know is what it is you're taking away from people. It's a piece of cultural identity that was once peaceful and charming and is now a brand of shame - even for those who really haven't benefited in any way from the atrocities.

There are some who would cite white privilege as the benefit. Why are a subset of the privileged being expected to hold the bulk of the guilt? Most of the south was left financially devastated from the civil war. There is no single sign of a child's chance for success in life than the<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1360943/"> social-economic status</a> within which that child is born. That is true no matter your race, gender, or level of ability.

The slave owners and the politicians who supported them deserved their fates, truly. Those born into the South of today do not deserve to bare that same fate. Within the context of the lowest social-economic status, the poor whites of the South may have it the "best". In the context of the nation, they do not. In the context of the nation, they have a distinct lack of privilege.

This demographic is disadvantaged but they are not recognized as such. They do have the privilege of blending in with the rest of white America. If he or she can keep his or her mouth shut, a poor southerner can get cleaned up, put on nice clothes, and blend in at the fancy cocktail party. However, behind that mask of privilege lies a lack of access to education, health care, and to be honest, emotional and social support. Because that mask can't be taken off. No matter how poor or desolate, the white mask remains. I'm not saying we need to acknowledge the South as a disadvantaged demographic. That's not at all where I'm going with this. My point is actually much much deeper, so hang on.

<strong>This urge</strong> to demonize the Confederate flag as a symbol of racism pacifies something in us. It gives guilty white minds a sense that they're pushing for some kind of change. It gives us an inanimate object to hate and blame. And maybe as we lay that flag to rest, some of our anger and hatred will go to rest with it. Is that enough?

<strong>This urge</strong>... when it's satisfied, what's changed? Once our white guilt is pacified, can we really truly believe we've done something other than blame it all on a flag? Not just a flag, but those southerners. Those dirty, backwards, southerners and their white privilege. If we blame them, are we free? If you're being that person, tell me... what does that really make you?

So just lay off a little, ok? Let's take the flag down, but embrace your Southerners. After all, we all still walk on the blood of the native inhabitants of our great country. If you don't understand what I'm talking about, I'm sure there are some southern Christians who can tell you a story about casting the first stone. They'll probably offer you some good iced-tea while they tell it to you. I may not buy into that whole hole-trinity-mythos thing, but that doesn't mean that the people who do aren't some of the kindest people I've known.

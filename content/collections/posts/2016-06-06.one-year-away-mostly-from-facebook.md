One year ago, shy a couple of weeks, I decided to stop using Facebook (FB). I saw my use of FB as an inhibitor in my life and set about making a change for what I expected to be the better. My experience did not meet my expectations. This blog post is a summary of my situation, motivations, expectations, and finally my lived experience.

It is important to consider my life situation leading up to the moment I decided to avoid using FB. I've also been a social media junky, well before term "social media" had been coined. I created my <a href="/blog/my-own-social-media-history">FB account in 2007</a> and was a frequent user for many years. In 2011, I became active in a Colorado non-profit with a very active Facebook community. I became a moderator and admin for a variety of groups and often found myself investing heavily in keeping the vibe chill in restless corners of cyberspace. But inside, inside I was... oh wait, I'll save that story for further down. The next important part of this narrative happened in 2014. That year, after I finished my Masters work at The Burning Man Project, I relocated from Boulder, CO to Seattle, WA. I've moved many times before in my life, but never have I left behind such a large group of cherished friends. From casual characters I might occasionally meet in public to my closest friends and lovers, they collectively created the most profound social experience out of any stage of my life thus far. I love them and I miss them. This move was also different for another reason. Every move I made in life before related to a personal employment opportunity. This time, I moved to Seattle in full support of my girlfriend as she pursued her dream job doing research on Alzheimer's at the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Institute_for_Brain_Science">Allen Institute for Brain Science</a>. I am dedicated to being here with her; for the first time in my career I am also confined to a small region of the world. At least it's an economically vibrant and visually stunning region of the world. In fact, I was able to pick up a programming contract and get to work fairly quickly. I had been at that new job just a couple of months when I made the decision to step away from Facebook.

At this point, perhaps you can already imagine my motivations. I found myself distracted at work because I wanted to connect with my friends on Facebook. However, it wasn't "my friends" that I kept "connecting" with. In fact, something entirely different was happening. No longer responsible for managing any of those Facebook groups, I was less concerned about being an appropriate role model. I started responding to posts, sometimes from people I didn't or barely recognized, and arguing about some fine point. Instead of connecting with friends, I was doing a whole lot of <a href="https://xkcd.com/386/">telling people that they were doing it wrong</a>. Sure, I was still engaging friends on FB often, but it was become less and less they key activity upon my visits. It wasn't ok, and it was impacting my real life and my attempts to develop myself socially in my new home. I wanted to break the addiction and put that energy towards positive personal development.

Have you seen  any of that self-help media that talk about how to turn yourself into an incredibly productivity machine until you've refined your own life into an art form? (I'm looking at you <a href="https://twitter.com/tferriss">Tim Ferriss.</a>) That's basically what I expected to happen. Not only would I excel in career, but I would build a vibrant family of friends all around me as well as a strong rapport across various communities. I would blog regularly and flesh out my online brand. I would do all of this because of the countless hours I saved by not burning off my cognitive surplus with meaningless engagements on Facebook.

So how did it work out for me? Well, I only wrote two blog posts during the entire year. A lot of Facebook energy ended up going into my <a href="https://twitter.com/zaskoda">Twitter Stream</a> instead. It doesn't provide the same level of instant validation that I experienced on FB, but it gives me a place to easily chronicle and share content and thoughts. It also gave me a unique chance to really focus on and "learn" about Twitter. For a service that is technically less complex than FB, the social constructs that have developed around it seem considerably more complex. The contrasts between these constructs would make for a great blog post, if only I could stop tweeting and write blog posts.

Aside from failing to become the hyper-productive variant of myself I had imagined, the experience of leaving Facebook took me down an unexpected path of emotions. I would have seen it coming if I had read <a href="https://www.reddit.com/r/getdisciplined/comments/2t2d8h/discussion_anyone_else_quit_facebook_whats_your/">threads like this one</a>. For the first month or three, it's euphoric. You really do feel the burst of energy that comes from cutting off an energy-sink that FB can be. This blissful honeymoon period comes to an end. Eventually you realize that you don't know what's going on in your friends' lives anymore. I struggled with that. My girlfriend, who was still an active user, had a much better idea of what  our friends were up to. I started to FB vicariously through her. Meanwhile, no matter how many times I shared my email address, phone number, and website address, a handful of friends and family would insist on messaging me through FB. On top of that, many of the new people I met here in Seattle were using FB to connect and, most of all, manage events. On top of missing my old friends, I was also missing the chance to better connect with new friends. Ultimately, I gave in and started <strong>visiting</strong> FB. I would lurk, but not like. I would reply only to direct messages. So when the title of this post says "mostly," it's because I've only kept myself cut off from engaging the public aspects of FB, such as the newsfeed.

Behind all of this, I have always had ethical issues with privately held social media platforms using our data in ways that we are likely going to regret in the long run. I want to another option to FB. I want tools that make it easy for me to engage those I care about without being exploited for advertisers. I don't want my platform to be designed specifically to keep me hooked. I am extremely scared about how we are building the hive mind. But nothing came. It's not because the technology never made it, but because the bees never left the old hive. We might all be using more than FB, but we all seem to still be using FB. I thought that after year of abstinence I would have broken my addiction and been ready to move on. Instead, I'm planning to come back.

I want to take what I've learned and create a better Facebook practice. This blog post is first step in that new practice. I intend to put more original content on my own website, where I have more control over my own data. It doesn't mean I won't post original stuff elsewhere. Rather, it means that I intend to make my website more of a cornerstone in my personal online communications methodology. I also intend to keep my controversial opinions out of the comment section of other people's posts. If I feel strongly inspired to respond to something outside of my own stream that may incite unfavorable responses, I want to invest that energy into creating substantive content on the topic somewhere in one of my own stream.

Instead of suggesting where this will go, I'll simply say that this feels like the right thing. Maybe a little later I'll write another blog post talking about how this new approach is working out. Thanks for taking the time to read.
